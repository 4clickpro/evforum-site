Home charging explained: what to ask an electrician before you buy

Published November 15, 2026 · EV Forum

For most electric car owners, home is where the charging happens. You plug in when you get home, and the car is ready in the morning — the same habit as charging a phone overnight. But the setup is worth sorting out before you buy the car, not after, because what your home can support decides how convenient that habit will be.

Most homes can charge an electric car in one way or another. The question is which way fits your home, your parking spot, and how far you drive each day — and that is exactly what a licensed electrician can answer in one visit.

The two ways to charge at home

At its simplest, an electric car can charge from a standard household outlet, using the portable charging cable that usually comes with the car. It works, and for someone who drives short distances and has plenty of time overnight, it can be enough. But it is slow, and for most drivers it is more of a backup than a plan.

The usual home setup is a dedicated charger, mounted on a garage wall or the outside of the house and wired into its own circuit. It charges noticeably faster than a standard outlet, is built for daily use in all weathers, and keeps the cable off the ground.

What the electrician will want to know

You do not need to know the technical details before the visit — that is the electrician's job. But a few facts about your home make the visit more useful.

  • Where the car parks. A garage, a driveway, or a parking space — and how far it is from your electrical panel. A short run of cable is simpler than a long one, and an outdoor charger needs weatherproofing a garage one does not.
  • What your electrical panel can handle. The panel is the box where power enters your home. Older panels sometimes need an upgrade before a charger can be added — only an electrician can judge that.
  • How you use electricity now. A home that already runs a lot of heavy equipment has less spare capacity than one that does not. The electrician will check what else draws power and whether the panel has room for another circuit.
  • Whether you rent or own. Renters need the landlord's permission before anything is installed, and some apartment buildings have their own rules about chargers in shared parking. Sort this out before spending money on a survey.

The questions to ask

When the electrician visits, these are the questions that matter most. Write down the answers — you will want them when you compare quotes.

  • Can my panel support a dedicated charger, or does it need an upgrade first? This is the most important question, because a panel upgrade changes the cost and the timescale of the whole job.
  • Where would you put the charger, and why there? The most practical spot is usually the one closest to both the panel and the car's charging port, with the cable reaching easily without lying across a walkway.
  • What does the full job include? A good quote covers the charger, the wiring, the circuit breaker, any trenching or drilling, and the inspection — not just the box on the wall.
  • Do I need a permit or an inspection? Rules vary by area, and in many places a new circuit needs a permit and a final inspection. Your electrician should know the local rules and handle the paperwork.
  • Is there anything that would make this job complicated? Detached garages, long cable runs, awkward panel locations and older wiring can all add time. Better to hear about them during the survey than halfway through the job.

Ask before you buy, not after

The reason to have this conversation before buying the car is simple: it removes the one surprise that can sour the first month of ownership. If the panel needs an upgrade, or the parking spot makes installation awkward, you want to know that while you are still choosing — not the week the car arrives and you discover you cannot charge it properly at home.

Most of the time the news is good: a short visit, a straightforward quote, and a charger on the wall before the car arrives. But even when the answer is more complicated, knowing early means you can plan for it. A home charger is a small job compared with buying a car — and it is the one that makes everything after it feel effortless.

Where to look next

For public charging while you are out, the networks in the EV Forum directory — ChargePoint, Electrify America and PlugShare — show where chargers are. For the car itself, check the official sites of the brands in the directory, such as Tesla, Lucid Motors and Polestar, for current specifications and charging details.

The short version

Home charging should be boring — plug in at night, drive in the morning. Get a licensed electrician to look at your panel and your parking spot before you buy, ask whether the panel can support a dedicated charger and what the full job includes, and get the answer in writing. Then the car arrives to a home that is already ready for it.
