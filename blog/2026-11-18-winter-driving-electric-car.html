Winter driving in an electric car: what changes and what to plan for

Published November 18, 2026 · EV Forum

The first cold snap is when many new electric car owners notice something different. The car still drives the same, but the range figure reads lower, the cabin takes longer to warm up, and a charging stop can take longer than in summer. None of that means anything is wrong — it is how batteries and heating work in the cold.

Why cold weather affects range

A battery works by moving energy through chemistry, and that chemistry slows down in the cold, so the same charge takes you less far than it would on a mild day. How much less depends on the car, the temperature and how you drive — the dashboard range figure is your car's own estimate, and it will move around as conditions change.

Heating is the other half of the story. A petrol car gets cabin heat as a by-product of the engine, more or less for free. An electric car has to spend battery energy to warm the cabin, and on a short trip that heating can use a noticeable share of the charge — which is why winter range feels shortest on short trips.

Don't worry about the exact number. Leave a bigger margin in winter: charge a little more, plan stops a little earlier, and treat the range figure as a rough guide rather than a promise.

Precondition while you are still plugged in

Most electric cars let you warm the battery and the cabin while the car is still plugged in at home, on a timer or from the car's app. Set off with a warm battery and a warm cabin, using energy from your home supply instead of the battery — the single most useful winter habit there is. If your car offers scheduled departure, set it up before the cold arrives so the car warms itself before you step out to a defrosted, clear-windowed car.

Heating the cabin without draining the battery

Heating the whole cabin to a high temperature uses a lot of energy, so use the heat where it counts. Seat heaters and a heated steering wheel warm you directly and use far less energy than heating all the air in the car — many owners find they can keep the cabin a degree or two lower and still feel comfortable with the seat heater on.

Clear snow and ice from the car before you set off, including the charging port door if it is iced shut. A few minutes with a scraper beats fighting a frozen flap at a public charger in the dark.

Tyres, grip and stopping distances

Winter driving is not only about the battery. Cold, wet and icy roads change grip, and stopping distances grow on every car, electric or not. If your area gets regular snow and ice, winter tyres are worth considering — their rubber compound stays flexible in the cold, which is what keeps grip when summer tyres go hard and slippery.

Keep an eye on tyre pressures too — air contracts as it gets colder, so pressures drop in winter. Check them when the tyres are cold, and top them up to the pressure on the sticker inside the driver's door, not the number printed on the tyre itself.

Electric cars are heavy and quick off the line, so on ice that instant torque can spin the wheels if you press the pedal hard. Ease away gently, leave more space than you think you need, and brake earlier than usual.

Charging in the cold

A cold battery charges more slowly than a warm one, especially at a fast public charger. That is normal: the car limits charging speed until the battery warms up. Driving for a while before you charge helps, as does setting the charger as your destination in the car's navigation, which on many cars warms the battery on the way there.

Plan for charging stops to take longer in winter, and do not arrive at a charger nearly empty — if one is out of order or busy, you want enough charge left to reach the next one.

Planning a winter trip

Winter trips need the same planning as any long electric journey, with more margin:

  • Check the route the night before. Know where the chargers are, and have a backup for each stop. Our guide to public charging explains how a session works.
  • Charge a bit more than you think you need. Extra minutes at a charger beat the worry of arriving nearly empty.
  • Warm the car while plugged in before you leave. It costs nothing from the battery and makes the first stretch easier.

The short version

Winter changes an electric car in three ways: the battery holds less usable energy when cold, heating the cabin uses battery energy, and charging takes longer. Plan for it: warm the car while it is plugged in, heat the seats rather than the whole cabin, keep your tyres right, and leave a bigger charging margin. Do those things and winter driving feels like ordinary driving — just with a coat on.
