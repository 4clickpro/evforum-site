How to read a range figure (and why two cars never match)

Published November 20, 2026 · EV Forum

Range is the number every electric car buyer looks at first, and it is also the number most often misunderstood. A range figure is not a promise about how far your car will go. It is the result of a standardised test, run in controlled conditions, so that different cars can be measured the same way.

What a range figure really is

When a maker publishes a range, it comes from a laboratory test cycle: the car is driven through a fixed pattern of speeds, stops and starts on a rolling road, in mild conditions, until the battery is empty. The test is the same for every car, which is the point — a common yardstick.

Different regions use different test cycles, and they do not give the same answers. A figure from one cycle is not directly comparable with one from another, so check which test a number comes from before you compare two cars. The maker's official site or the official test body's published figure is the source to trust, not a headline or an advert.

Why your range will differ from the figure

Real driving is not a laboratory. Speed is the biggest factor: driving steadily at motorway speeds uses far more energy than the gentle, stop-start pattern of a test cycle, so long fast journeys will fall short of the published figure. Short trips in town, with lots of slowing down and regenerative braking, can come closer — or even beat it on a mild day.

Weather matters too. Cold air makes the battery less efficient and heating the cabin draws energy, so winter range is lower than summer range. Rain, wind and a loaded car all add their share. None of this means the car is faulty — it is simply how electric cars behave.

Then there is the way you drive. Hard acceleration and late braking use more energy; smooth driving and using the car's eco or regenerative settings use less. Two people driving the same car on the same route can get noticeably different results, which is why one owner's experience is never a reliable guide to what you will get.

Why two cars with the same figure are not the same

Two cars can carry the same headline range and behave very differently. One may have a large battery and modest efficiency; the other a smaller battery and much better efficiency. The first will take longer to charge, while the second may be lighter and cheaper to run — even though the headline number is identical.

Options change the figure too. Larger wheels, a roof box or a different trim level can all move the tested range, so the figure for the base model may not match the car you actually configure. Always check the range for the exact version you are buying, not the one in the launch photos.

A range figure also says nothing about charging speed. A long-range car that charges slowly can be less convenient on a trip than a shorter-range car that charges quickly. Range and charging are two different questions, and both matter.

How to use the figure when you shop

Start with your own week, not the brochure. Work out your longest regular journey — the commute, the weekend trip, the visit to family — and how often you would need to charge for it. If your longest regular drive sits comfortably inside the car's real-world range, with room to spare for cold weather and a detour, the figure is doing its job.

Then compare cars on the same test cycle, for the same trim, and treat the figure as a ranking tool rather than a guarantee. A car rated higher than another on the same test will generally go further in the same conditions — but the gap in your hands may differ from the gap on paper.

Finally, take a test drive that includes the kind of road you actually use. A drive that mixes town streets and faster roads, with the heating or cooling on, tells you far more than a short loop around the dealer. Ask the dealer what real owners typically see in your area, and treat any single answer as a rough guide, not a fact.

A short checklist before you compare

  • Check the test. Same cycle, same trim, or the comparison means nothing.
  • Expect less than the figure. Motorway speed, cold weather and heating all reduce it.
  • Think about your longest regular trip. That is the range that matters, not the best-case number.
  • Look past the headline. Efficiency, battery size and charging speed shape the real experience.
  • Check the official source. The maker's published figure for your exact version is the one to use.

The short version

A range figure is a standard test result, not a promise — it lets you compare cars on equal terms, but your speed, the weather and how you drive move the real number up and down. Compare like with like, plan around your longest regular trip, and remember that charging speed matters just as much as range.
