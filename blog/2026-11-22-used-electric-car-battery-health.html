Used electric car battery health: what to check before you buy

Published November 22, 2026 · EV Forum

On a used petrol car you listen to the engine and check for leaks. On a used electric car the most expensive part is hidden under the floor, sealed away where you cannot see it: the battery. It is the one thing to understand before you hand over any money. Checking it is not guesswork: there is a report to ask for, clues the car itself gives you, and a warranty to check.

Why the battery matters most

A battery slowly loses some of its capacity as it ages and is used. That is normal for every electric car, in the same way tyres wear. The question is never whether a used battery has lost anything — it is how much, and whether the car still does the job you need it to do. A healthy battery charges normally and behaves the same from one week to the next; a struggling one may charge slowly, lose range unexpectedly, or show warning lights.

Ask for a battery health report

The single most useful document when buying a used electric car is a battery health report — a diagnostic check, usually by a dealer or a specialist, giving a state-of-health reading: how much of the battery's original usable capacity remains.

Ask the seller for one before you commit to anything. A dealer selling used electric cars should be able to provide a recent report, or to get one done. If the seller cannot or will not provide one, treat that as information in itself. When you have the report, check that it is recent, names the car it was taken from, and comes from a named source — the dealer, the manufacturer, or an independent specialist — rather than a number quoted from memory.

There is no single "good" number

It is tempting to look for one magic figure that means a battery is fine. There is not one. What counts as healthy depends on the age of the car, how many miles it has covered, and the model itself. A battery in an older, high-mileage car will naturally read lower than one in a nearly new car, and that can still be fine for the price. The useful question is not "is this number high enough?" but "does this reading match the car, the age, the mileage and the price?"

Check the battery warranty, in writing

Most electric cars carry a separate warranty for the battery, on top of the general warranty for the rest of the car. It is usually longer, and one of the most valuable things a used electric car can still have. Ask for the exact terms for this car, in writing: how long the battery cover lasts, whether it transfers to a new owner, and what it covers. Do not rely on a verbal promise — get the document and read it.

What the test drive can tell you

If you can, charge the car yourself — at a home charger or a public one — and see that a session starts normally and the battery percentage climbs steadily. Check the dashboard for any battery or charging warnings before and after the drive. Then drive it as you would actually use it: town streets and a faster road. The range estimate changes with speed, weather and driving style, so do not judge it against a single figure from a brochure — judge it against whether it behaves sensibly and consistently. If the range drops far faster than the miles you are covering, or the car limits its own power, get the car checked before you buy.

Get an independent check

If you are serious about the car, pay for a pre-purchase inspection by someone who knows electric cars — a specialist or a dealer for that make, since a general mechanic may not have the diagnostic tools for a battery check. An inspection costs money, but it is a small amount next to the price of the car, and good protection against buying a problem. If the seller will not let you have the car inspected, walk away.

A short checklist before you buy

  • Get the battery health report. Recent, naming the car, from a named source.
  • Match the report to the car. Age, mileage and price should all make sense together.
  • Ask about charging history. How it was charged, any battery work, any warnings shown.
  • Check the battery warranty in writing. Length, transfer to a new owner, and what it covers.
  • Charge it yourself on the test drive. Watch a session start and the percentage climb steadily.
  • Watch for warnings. Any battery or charging warning light is a reason to pause and get the car checked.
  • Get an independent inspection. By someone with the tools to read a battery properly.

The short version

Battery health is the one thing you cannot judge by looking at a used electric car — so do not try. Get the report, check the warranty in writing, charge the car yourself on the test drive, and have a specialist look it over. A car that passes those checks is one you can buy with confidence.
