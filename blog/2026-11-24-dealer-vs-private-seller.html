Dealer vs private seller: what changes when you buy an electric car

Published November 24, 2026 · EV Forum

There are two main ways to buy an electric car: from a dealer, or from a private seller. The car can be the same either way, but almost everything around it changes — the price, the protection if something goes wrong, who sorts the paperwork, and what you need to check first.

What a dealer gives you

A dealer is a business, and that changes the deal. A dealer will usually have checked the car over before selling it, and many will offer some kind of warranty or guarantee — what that covers, and for how long, varies, so ask for it in writing and read what it actually includes. Dealers also handle the paperwork: the title transfer, the registration, and checks for any money still owed on the car. Some will take your old car in part exchange, or help you sort out home charging — ask about both rather than assuming.

All of that is built into the price, usually higher than a private sale for a similar car. What you are paying for is the dealer's preparation, the paperwork, and the safety net if something goes wrong soon after you buy. Consumer protection rules vary by place, so check the current rules for your area from an official source before you rely on them.

What a private seller gives you

A private seller is usually selling one car — their own. The price is often lower than a dealer's for a similar car, because there is no preparation, no warranty and no showroom to pay for. You also get to talk to the person who actually drove it: how it was charged, where it was kept, why they are selling, and how it behaved day to day.

The trade-off is that a private sale is usually sold as seen. Once you have bought it, the car is yours, faults and all, and getting help afterwards is much harder. A private sale is not a bad idea — it just means the checks you do before you buy matter far more, because there is no safety net.

The battery question matters more with a private sale

The battery is the most expensive part of an electric car, so it deserves the most attention — and doubly so in a private sale. Ask the seller for a battery health report, and read our guide on used electric car battery health before you go to look at the car. Ask whether the battery warranty still applies and whether it transfers to a new owner, and get the answer in writing. A dealer should know this straight away; a private seller may not, which is itself useful information. If the seller has no battery information at all, treat that as a reason to pause.

What to check, whichever way you buy

Some checks are the same no matter who sells you the car. Drive it on roads you know, and charge it yourself before you buy — at home if you can, or at a public charger — so you know the port and cable work. Check the charging cables are included and the service history is there to see. Look at the tyres, brakes and bodywork as you would with any used car. And check the paperwork: the title or registration document, the service records, and proof of who owns the car. If anything is missing, or the seller cannot explain it, walk away. There will be another car.

Paperwork, payment and staying safe

With a dealer, the paperwork is usually handled for you. With a private seller, it is yours to sort out: the title transfer, the registration in your name, and checking there is no money still owed on the car. The rules vary by place, so check the official process for your area before you buy, and do not hand over money until you know what documents you should receive. Pay in a way that leaves a record, meet in a safe public place, and never pay in advance for a car you have not seen. If a deal feels rushed, or the seller will not let you inspect the car properly, walk away.

A short checklist before you pay

  • Decide what matters more: a lower price, or a safety net.
  • Ask for any warranty in writing — what it covers, and for how long.
  • Get a battery health report and check the battery warranty transfers to you.
  • Charge the car yourself before you buy.
  • Check the title, service history and ownership documents.
  • Sort the title transfer and registration before you pay.
  • If anything is missing or unexplained, walk away.

The short version

The car is the same either way; the deal is not. A dealer costs more and gives you preparation, paperwork and a safety net. A private seller costs less and gives you the car's real history, but no safety net — so your own checks have to do the work. Whichever way you buy, get the battery report, the charging test and the paperwork right.
