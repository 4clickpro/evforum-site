Electric car maintenance: what actually needs servicing

Published November 26, 2026 · EV Forum

Electric cars need less maintenance than petrol cars — mostly. There is no engine oil to change, no exhaust to rust, and no gearbox full of moving parts. But less maintenance is not no maintenance. An electric car is still a heavy car with tyres, brakes, suspension and a battery, and the parts that do need attention are easy to overlook. Here is what actually needs servicing, and what you can check yourself.

What an electric car does not have

There is no oil change, no spark plugs, no fuel filter and no exhaust system. The drive unit has far fewer moving parts than a petrol or diesel engine, so there is simply less to wear out — and that is where most of the maintenance saving comes from. The work that matters moves somewhere else: to the tyres, the brakes, the small battery, and the parts every car shares.

Tyres wear first

Tyres are the part of an electric car that works hardest. The car is heavy, and the instant torque of an electric motor puts more load through the tyres. Check the tread and the pressure regularly — under-inflated tyres wear faster and use more energy — and have the tyres rotated on the schedule in the owner's manual, so they wear evenly. When the time comes to replace them, the owner's manual or a tyre specialist can confirm what fits your car.

Brakes are used less — and still need checking

Regenerative braking does much of the slowing down in an electric car, feeding energy back into the battery instead of turning it into heat at the brakes. So the pads and discs can last much longer. But brakes that sit unused can corrode, so they still need inspecting at every service. If the brakes feel rough, noisy or grabby, or the car has been standing for weeks, have them looked at rather than assuming they are fine.

The small battery most people forget

Every electric car has a second, ordinary 12-volt battery alongside the big traction battery. It powers the lights, the locks, the computers and the systems that wake the car up. Like any 12-volt battery, it can go flat — and when it does, the car may not start even though the main battery is full. Know where it is, watch for slow starts or warning messages, and replace it when it weakens rather than waiting for it to fail.

Filters, wipers and fluids

The cabin air filter is usually a simple replacement at the service interval. Wiper blades, washer fluid and lights are the same as on any car — check them, top them up, and replace them when they wear. The battery cooling system is sealed — it is checked as part of the service, not topped up by you.

Software is part of servicing now

Many electric cars receive software updates over the air, improving charging, the range display or the driver assistance features. Keep them installed, and read what each one changes before you drive. Some updates need the car parked and charging, so check the conditions in the update notice. Keep the car's app and your charging apps up to date too.

Follow the manufacturer's schedule

The simplest rule is to follow the service schedule in the owner's manual. Intervals are usually longer than for petrol cars, but they still exist, and keeping to them protects the warranty — some warranties require servicing at a franchised dealer or an approved specialist, so check the terms before you book anywhere else. A service is also when a technician checks the things you cannot: the battery's cooling system, the high-voltage cables and the underbody. Do not skip one just because the car feels fine.

What to leave to a specialist

Anything to do with the high-voltage battery, the drive unit or the charging system belongs to a trained specialist. Never open the battery casing, cut a high-voltage cable or try to repair a charging fault yourself — the voltages involved are dangerous, and work on them needs the right training and equipment. If you see a warning light about the battery or charging, or the car refuses to charge, book it in and describe the warning exactly.

A short checklist

  • Check tyre tread and pressure regularly, and rotate tyres on the manual's schedule.
  • Have the brakes inspected at every service, even if they feel fine.
  • Know where the 12-volt battery is, and replace it when it weakens.
  • Replace the cabin filter, wipers and washer fluid as they wear.
  • Install software updates when they arrive.
  • Follow the manufacturer's service schedule — and check the warranty terms first.
  • Leave anything high-voltage to a trained specialist.

The short version

An electric car needs less servicing than a petrol car, but the servicing it does need is easy to forget. Tyres, brakes, the 12-volt battery and the service schedule are the things that matter — and the high-voltage parts are for a specialist, never a do-it-yourself job. Keep up with those, and the car will look after itself.
