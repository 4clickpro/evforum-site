Charging at work: what to ask your employer

Published November 27, 2026 · EV Forum

For many drivers, the workplace is the missing piece of the charging puzzle. If you cannot charge at home — a flat, a rented house, a street without a driveway — a charger at work can do the same job: the car sits in the car park all day anyway, and it can be charging while you work. But workplace charging is not one thing — some employers offer it free, some charge for it, and some have nothing yet. Before you buy an electric car on the strength of it, ask the questions below.

First: does your employer offer it at all?

Start with whoever looks after the building or the car park — facilities, estates, or the office manager — rather than guessing. Some workplaces have chargers that are easy to miss, tucked in a corner of the car park or reserved for a particular team. Ask whether chargers exist, where they are, and who is allowed to use them. Some are open to all staff, some are for fleet vehicles only. If there is nothing yet, ask whether anything is planned.

Who pays for the electricity?

This is the question that matters most for your weekly costs. Some employers provide charging free as a staff benefit. Others charge for the electricity — a flat rate, a charge by the energy used, or billing through an app. Ask how it works in practice: do you pay the employer, or a charging network? Get the answer in writing if you can, because a free charger that later becomes a paid one changes the maths of owning the car. Tax treatment of workplace charging can vary by place, so check the current rules for your area from an official source.

How fast are the chargers, and how long can you stay plugged in?

Workplace chargers are usually the slower kind — designed for a car that sits there for hours, not minutes. That is fine if you are parked all day. Ask whether there is a limit on how long one car can stay connected: many workplaces run a move-on rule, where you move your car once it is charged so someone else can use the bay. A charger you cannot get to is no use at all.

How many chargers, and how busy are they?

Two chargers shared between fifty electric cars is a very different offer from ten shared between five. Ask how many charging bays there are, how many electric cars already use them, and whether there is a booking system or a rota. If the chargers are already busy at the times you would need them, that tells you something important before you commit to a car.

How do you actually start a charge?

Ask what you need to use the chargers: an app, a charge card, an employee badge, or just a plug and a socket. Some workplace chargers are on a public network, which means you need an account with that network and may pay its rates. Others are private to the building and work with a staff card. Find out before your first day with the car. Also ask who to call when a charger is broken — one that has been out of order for a month with nobody responsible is a common story.

If there is nothing yet, how do you make the case?

If your employer has no chargers, a single polite request rarely gets them installed — but a small group of staff asking together often does. Find out how many colleagues already drive electric, or are thinking about it. Employers tend to listen when the demand is visible and the request is specific: a small number of chargers, a trial period, a survey of staff. Incentives and grants for workplace charging exist in some places and change over time, so check the current schemes for your area from an official source and pass what you find to whoever handles the building.

A short checklist

  • Ask whether chargers exist, where they are, and who can use them.
  • Find out who pays — free, charged, or split — and how billing works.
  • Check the speed and the time limit, and whether there is a move-on rule.
  • Count the chargers against the cars that already use them.
  • Learn how to start a charge — app, card, badge — and who fixes a broken charger.
  • If there is nothing yet, gather colleagues and make a specific, small ask.
  • Check the tax and incentive rules for your area from an official source.

The short version

Workplace charging can be the answer if home charging is not an option — but only if you know how it actually works where you work. Ask who pays, how fast it is, how busy it gets, and how you start a charge, and if there is nothing yet, ask with colleagues rather than alone. A charger you can rely on every working day is worth more than a fast one you can only reach once a week.
