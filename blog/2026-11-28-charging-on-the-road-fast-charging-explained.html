Charging on the road: fast charging explained

Published November 28, 2026 · EV Forum

Home charging covers most of an electric car's life — the car charges overnight and you start each day with a full battery. Fast charging is for the rest: the long journey, the unexpected detour, the day you forgot to plug in. It worries new owners most — and turns out to be simplest once you have done it once.

What makes a charger "fast"?

A home charger supplies alternating current, and the car converts it to the direct current the battery needs — a slow, steady process that suits a car parked for hours. A fast charger does the conversion itself and feeds direct current straight into the battery, which is why it can add a useful amount of range in the time of a coffee stop. You will find them where people stop on a journey: motorway service areas, supermarket car parks, petrol stations and town centres.

Why the speed changes during a session

The first surprise for most new owners is that fast charging is not one steady speed. A session usually starts quickly, then slows down as the battery fills. That is normal: a battery accepts energy most easily when it is partly empty, and more cautiously as it approaches full. The practical result is that the last stretch of a charge takes far longer than the first — charging from nearly empty to about four-fifths full is quick, while topping up the final stretch takes much longer for very little extra range. On a long journey, that is why experienced drivers stop more often and charge less each time: two shorter stops can be quicker overall than one long one that ends with the car trickling slowly towards full.

What affects how fast your car charges

Several things decide the speed you actually get. Your car has its own maximum charging speed, set by its manufacturer, and no charger can push past it — so the speed on the screen is whichever is lower, the car's limit or the charger's. The battery's state matters too: a cold battery charges more slowly than a warm one, which is why winter charging can feel sluggish at first and then pick up as the battery warms. Many cars warm the battery on the way if you set the charger as your destination in the car's navigation. Some sites also share power between bays, so if two cars are plugged into the same unit, each may charge more slowly than one car on its own. It is not a fault — it is how the site is built.

How you pay and start a charge

Starting a fast charge is usually one of three ways: tap a bank card on the charger's reader, use the charging network's app, or tap a charge card from that network. Many newer chargers take a contactless card payment, so you do not need an account at all. Smaller sites may need the network's app, so have the main apps on your phone before a long trip. Prices vary by network, by site, and sometimes by the time of day, and they change over time — so check the price on the charger's screen or in the network's app before you start. Either way, the screen should show the cost before you commit.

How to find a fast charger on the road

Your car's built-in navigation is the simplest place to start: most electric cars can show chargers along a route and plan stops for you, including how long to charge at each one. For a second opinion, apps such as PlugShare list chargers from many networks in one place, with recent reports from other drivers about whether a charger is working. The networks in the EV Forum directory, including ChargePoint and Electrify America, also show their own chargers and live status in their apps.

A short checklist

  • Know your car's connector — check the manual or the charging port first.
  • Plan your stops before you leave, with a backup charger for each one.
  • Arrive with the battery fairly low — the first part is the quickest.
  • Charge to what you need, not to full — the last stretch is the slowest.
  • Have a payment method ready — a contactless card, or the network's app.
  • Check the price on the screen before you start, and keep the receipt.
  • Move on when you are done — someone else may be waiting for the bay.
  • In cold weather, set the charger as your destination so the battery warms up on the way.

The short version

Fast charging is simply a bigger charger feeding energy straight into the battery, so a stop on a journey adds a useful amount of range while you stretch your legs. It starts quickly and slows as the battery fills, so charging to what you need — rather than to full — is usually quickest. Plan your stops, keep a backup in mind, and it becomes routine after the first couple of times.
